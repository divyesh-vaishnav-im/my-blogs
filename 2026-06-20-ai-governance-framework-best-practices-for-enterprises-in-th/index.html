AI Governance Framework: Best Practices for Enterprises in the Age of AI-Driven Cyber Threats

Artificial intelligence is no longer a futuristic concept sitting in research labs — it's embedded in enterprise workflows, customer-facing products, cloud infrastructure, and increasingly, in the hands of sophisticated threat actors. As organizations accelerate AI adoption, a critical question has moved to the boardroom: Who governs AI, and how?

The answer isn't just a technology problem. It's a governance imperative.

India's Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) addressed this directly in its AI Cyber Defence Blueprint (Version 1.0, May 2025), laying out a comprehensive cybersecurity governance and operational readiness framework that enterprises must adopt to defend against AI-assisted threats. This article unpacks that framework, translates it into actionable enterprise guidance, and provides CISOs, IT leaders, compliance teams, and architects with a practical roadmap for building a mature AI governance posture.

"Cybersecurity governance and organisational readiness should be continuously reviewed and updated to address evolving AI-assisted cyber threats, changing technology environments, and organisational risk exposure." — CERT-In AI Cyber Defence Blueprint, 2025

Let's dive deep.

Why AI Governance Is a Board-Level Priority Today

The threat landscape has fundamentally shifted. Adversaries are now leveraging AI to craft hyper-personalized phishing emails, generate deepfake audio and video for impersonation attacks, automate vulnerability discovery, and evade traditional detection systems. At the same time, enterprises are deploying AI models that access sensitive data, make autonomous decisions, and integrate with third-party APIs — often without adequate oversight.

This dual reality — AI as both a weapon and a business tool — creates a governance gap that attackers actively exploit. Shadow AI deployments, ungoverned model integrations, and absent usage policies leave organizations exposed to risks that traditional cybersecurity frameworks weren't designed to handle.

According to the CERT-In blueprint, effective defense against AI-assisted threats requires strong governance, defined accountability, continuous risk assessment, and organisational preparedness. These aren't checkbox compliance activities — they are operational necessities.

The 12 Pillars of an Enterprise AI Governance Framework

The CERT-In framework identifies twelve distinct governance areas that together form a comprehensive AI and cybersecurity governance architecture. Here's how enterprises should interpret and implement each one.

1. Leadership Oversight and Accountability

Governance without executive ownership is theater. The first pillar demands that leadership — from the CISO to the CEO and board — have direct visibility into cybersecurity and AI-related risks. This means establishing a governance structure with clear executive review cadences, defined risk ownership at the C-suite level, and cross-functional coordination mechanisms that bring together IT, legal, compliance, HR, and business units.

Practical action: Create an AI Risk Committee that reports directly to the board. Assign a named executive (e.g., Chief AI Officer or CISO) as the accountable owner for AI governance outcomes. Ensure resource allocation for AI security is a standing agenda item in quarterly business reviews.

2. Cybersecurity Governance Framework

A formal governance framework provides the structural backbone for all security activities. This pillar covers security policies, risk management processes, incident governance, escalation mechanisms, and assurance processes — all aligned with organizational and regulatory requirements.

For enterprises operating in regulated sectors (banking, healthcare, critical infrastructure), this alignment must extend to frameworks like ISO/IEC 27001, NIST CSF, RBI cybersecurity guidelines, and CERT-In directives. The governance framework should not be a static document — it must be a living system with defined review cycles.

Suggested diagram: A governance hierarchy flowchart showing the board → AI Risk Committee → CISO → Security Operations → Business Units, with feedback loops at each level.

3. AI Governance and Responsible AI Usage

This is the most enterprise-specific pillar and arguably the most urgent. Organizations must govern how AI systems are used internally — not just how they defend against external AI threats. The CERT-In blueprint specifically calls out the need for AI usage policies, AI risk assessments, shadow AI monitoring, and governance of AI integrations.

Shadow AI — employees using unauthorized AI tools like consumer-grade chatbots, image generators, or code assistants — is one of the most underestimated risks in enterprises today. Sensitive data entered into unvetted AI platforms can be exposed, retained, or used for model training by third parties.

Practical action:

  • Publish a formal AI Acceptable Use Policy (AUP) that defines approved tools, prohibited use cases, and data handling requirements.
  • Deploy network monitoring or DLP solutions to detect shadow AI usage.
  • Require AI risk assessments before any new AI tool or model is integrated into business workflows.
  • Establish an AI model inventory — a registry of all AI systems in use, their data inputs, outputs, and risk classifications.

4. Risk Management and Exposure Assessment

Continuous risk assessment is non-negotiable in an AI-driven threat environment. The CERT-In framework recommends using xBOM (Extended Bill of Materials) — which encompasses Software BOM (SBOM), AI BOM (AIBOM), and data lineage inventories — to maintain comprehensive visibility into digital assets and their dependencies.

Exposure reviews must cover not just internal systems but also third-party dependencies, cloud services, and AI model supply chains. An AI model trained on compromised data or sourced from an untrusted vendor represents a systemic risk that traditional asset inventories miss entirely.

Industry use case: A financial services firm conducting quarterly exposure reviews discovered that a third-party fraud detection AI model was sending anonymized transaction data to an overseas server for retraining — a contractual and regulatory violation that only emerged through a structured xBOM review and vendor reassessment process.

5. Policy and Standards Management

Policies are only as effective as their enforcement. This pillar covers IAM policies, cloud security standards, secure development practices, AI usage controls, and incident reporting procedures. For AI-specific governance, enterprises need policies that address:

  • Model access controls (who can deploy, modify, or retire AI models)
  • Data governance for AI training datasets
  • Prompt injection and adversarial input protections for AI-integrated applications
  • Mandatory incident reporting when AI systems behave anomalously

6. Organisational Roles and Responsibilities

Ambiguity in ownership is a governance failure. Every AI system and cybersecurity function must have a named owner, defined escalation path, and clear operational responsibilities. This extends to third-party oversight — vendors and managed service providers must have contractually defined security responsibilities.

Suggested table: A RACI matrix mapping AI governance activities (risk assessment, policy enforcement, incident response, vendor oversight) to roles (CISO, AI Owner, Legal, Compliance, Business Unit Head, Third-Party Manager).

7. Workforce Awareness and Capacity Building

The human layer remains the most targeted attack surface. AI-powered phishing, deepfake impersonation, and voice cloning attacks are now sophisticated enough to fool trained professionals. The CERT-In framework emphasizes security awareness programs, deepfake awareness training, role-based training, and incident reporting awareness.

Generic annual security training is insufficient. Enterprises need role-specific simulations — finance teams trained to detect AI-generated CFO voice fraud, HR teams trained to identify deepfake video interview scams, and IT teams trained to recognize AI-assisted social engineering targeting privileged access.

Practical action: Run quarterly AI-specific phishing simulations. Include deepfake detection modules in security awareness programs. Establish a simple, low-friction incident reporting channel so employees can flag suspicious AI-generated content without fear of blame.

8. Third-Party and Supply-Chain Governance

AI supply chain risk is an emerging and underappreciated threat vector. Enterprises depend on AI models, APIs, cloud AI services, and software libraries — each representing a potential point of compromise. The CERT-In blueprint recommends xBOMs, vendor assessments, contractual controls, dependency visibility, and supplier reassessment as core measures.

A compromised AI model embedded in a software supply chain can introduce backdoors, bias, or data exfiltration capabilities that are extremely difficult to detect post-deployment. The SolarWinds attack demonstrated how supply chain compromise can cascade across thousands of organizations — AI supply chains face the same structural vulnerability.

Practical action: Require AI vendors to provide AI BOMs (AIBOMs) disclosing training data sources, model architecture, and third-party dependencies. Include AI-specific security clauses in vendor contracts. Conduct annual supplier reassessments with AI-specific security questionnaires.

9. Security Metrics and Governance Reporting

You cannot govern what you cannot measure. This pillar calls for tracking vulnerability metrics, incident response timelines, exposure trends, monitoring coverage, and governance review completion rates. For AI governance specifically, enterprises should track:

  • Number of AI systems in the approved inventory vs. detected shadow AI instances
  • AI risk assessment completion rate across business units
  • Mean time to detect and respond to AI-related security incidents
  • Third-party AI vendor assessment completion and remediation rates
  • Training completion rates for AI-specific security awareness programs

These metrics should feed into monthly governance dashboards presented to the AI Risk Committee and quarterly reports to the board.

10. Incident Governance and Escalation Readiness

AI-assisted cyberattacks move faster and scale more aggressively than traditional attacks. Incident governance must account for this velocity. The framework requires escalation procedures, executive communication protocols, evidence preservation processes, and regulatory coordination.

For AI-specific incidents — such as a compromised AI model producing malicious outputs, a deepfake-enabled business email compromise, or an AI-assisted ransomware attack — response playbooks must be pre-defined and tested. Waiting until an incident occurs to figure out escalation paths is a governance failure that attackers count on.

11. Security Assurance and Continuous Assessment

The CERT-In blueprint specifically recommends continuous cybersecurity audits from CERT-In empanelled Auditing Organisations, aligned with the Comprehensive Cyber Security Audit Policy Guidelines. This goes beyond annual penetration testing — it encompasses control validation, risk reviews, and compliance assessments on an ongoing basis.

For AI governance, this means regularly auditing AI model behavior, testing adversarial robustness, validating data privacy controls, and assessing whether AI systems are operating within their defined governance boundaries. Red team exercises that simulate AI-assisted attacks against enterprise defenses should be conducted at least annually.

12. Continuous Improvement and Adaptive Readiness

The final pillar recognizes that AI governance is not a destination — it's a continuous journey. Threat intelligence must be integrated into governance reviews. Operational learnings from incidents, near-misses, and tabletop exercises must feed back into policy and control updates. Resilience enhancement is an ongoing program, not a project with an end date.

Practical action: Establish a quarterly AI governance review cycle that incorporates the latest threat intelligence, regulatory updates, and operational learnings. Assign a dedicated team or function responsible for tracking AI threat developments and translating them into governance updates.

Implementation Maturity: Where Does Your Enterprise Stand?

Not all enterprises start from the same baseline. A useful way to approach AI governance implementation is through a maturity model:

  • Level 1 – Initial: Ad hoc AI usage, no formal policies, reactive incident response, limited executive awareness.
  • Level 2 – Developing: Basic AI usage policies exist, some risk assessments conducted, incident response plans drafted but untested.
  • Level 3 – Defined: Formal AI governance framework in place, AI inventory maintained, regular training conducted, vendor assessments performed.
  • Level 4 – Managed: Metrics-driven governance, continuous monitoring, integrated threat intelligence, tested incident playbooks, board-level reporting.
  • Level 5 – Optimizing: Adaptive governance with real-time AI risk visibility, automated compliance checks, proactive threat modeling, continuous improvement cycles.

Most enterprises currently operate between Level 1 and Level 2 for AI-specific governance. The CERT-In framework provides a clear pathway to Level 3 and beyond.

Common Challenges and How to Overcome Them

Challenge 1: Shadow AI proliferation. Business units adopt AI tools faster than governance teams can assess them. Solution: Implement a lightweight AI tool approval process (2-week fast track for low-risk tools) and deploy network-level monitoring to detect unauthorized AI service usage.

Challenge 2: Lack of executive buy-in. AI governance is seen as an IT problem, not a business priority. Solution: Frame AI governance in business risk terms — regulatory fines, reputational damage, operational disruption — and present board-level metrics that connect governance maturity to risk reduction.

Challenge 3: Third-party opacity. Vendors resist disclosing AI model details or supply chain information. Solution: Make AIBOM disclosure and AI security questionnaire completion contractual requirements. Leverage procurement leverage to enforce compliance.

Challenge 4: Skills gap. Security teams lack AI-specific expertise to assess model risks or detect AI-assisted attacks. Solution: Invest in role-based AI security training, partner with CERT-In empanelled auditors with AI expertise, and consider hiring or contracting AI security specialists.

Key Takeaways for Enterprise Leaders

The CERT-In AI Cyber Defence Blueprint provides a rigorous, structured foundation for enterprise AI governance. Here are the non-negotiable actions every enterprise should prioritize:

  1. Establish executive-owned AI governance with board-level visibility and cross-functional accountability.
  2. Deploy an AI usage policy and shadow AI monitoring before your next business unit deploys an unauthorized AI tool.
  3. Build and maintain an AI inventory (AIBOM) covering all internal and third-party AI systems.
  4. Conduct AI-specific risk assessments for every new AI integration, including supply chain dependencies.
  5. Train your workforce on AI-enabled threats — deepfakes, AI phishing, and voice cloning — with role-specific simulations.
  6. Engage CERT-In empanelled auditors for continuous security assurance aligned with national guidelines.
  7. Treat AI governance as a continuous program, not a compliance project — integrate threat intelligence and operational learnings into regular governance review cycles.

Conclusion: Governance Is Your First Line of AI Defense

In the AI era, the organizations that will weather cyber threats most effectively are not necessarily those with the most sophisticated technology — they are those with the strongest governance. Technology without governance is a liability. Governance without technology is insufficient. Together, they form the foundation of a resilient enterprise.

The CERT-In AI Cyber Defence Blueprint's twelve-pillar governance framework is not aspirational guidance — it is a practical, implementable roadmap that CISOs, IT leaders, and enterprise decision-makers can act on today. The enterprises that treat AI governance as a strategic priority — rather than a compliance afterthought — will be the ones that can harness AI's transformative potential while keeping adversaries at bay.

Start with leadership accountability. Build your AI inventory. Train your people. Govern your vendors. Measure everything. And never stop adapting.

The threat is evolving. Your governance must evolve faster.
